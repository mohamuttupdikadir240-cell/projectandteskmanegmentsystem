ABSTRACT
Effective coordination of projects and tasks remains a persistent challenge for many organizations that still rely on manual methods such as spreadsheets, paper-based logs, and informal messaging to plan, assign, and track work. These methods are prone to miscommunication, lost updates, missed deadlines, and a lack of visibility into who is responsible for what, which ultimately reduces organizational productivity and accountability. This study set out to design and implement a role-based, web-based Project and Task Management System capable of centralizing project planning, task assignment, progress tracking, deadline management, and reporting in a single platform accessible to Administrators, Project Managers, and Team Members.
The study adopted the Agile-oriented Software Development Life Cycle (incremental development) as its research and system development design, combined with a Clean Architecture approach for the system's technical design. Requirements were gathered through review of existing literature and analysis of common organizational project-management practices, and were translated into functional and non-functional requirements, entity-relationship models, and use-case designs. The resulting system was implemented using ASP.NET Core 9 MVC, Entity Framework Core 9, and Microsoft SQL Server, and provides role-scoped dashboards, project and task CRUD operations, team assignment, comments and file attachments, automated deadline notifications, exportable PDF/Excel reports, analytics, audit logging, and global search.
The system was tested using unit and integration tests to verify that each module performs its intended function and that the modules interact correctly end-to-end. The findings show that a centralized, role-based platform can substantially reduce the coordination overhead associated with manual project tracking, improve the traceability of task ownership, and give managers real-time visibility into project and team performance. The study concludes with recommendations for further enhancement of the system, including native mobile access, real-time push notifications, and integration with third-party calendar and communication tools.
Keywords: Project Management, Task Management, Role-Based Access Control, ASP.NET Core, Clean Architecture, Software Development Life Cycle.

CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background of the Study
Globally, the growth of organizations across sectors such as construction, information technology, non-governmental work, banking and education has been accompanied by an increasing volume and complexity of projects that must be planned, executed and monitored within limited time and resources. Project management, understood as the application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities in order to meet defined requirements, has therefore become a central organizational function (Project Management Institute, 2021). As organizations scale, the number of concurrent projects, tasks and contributors grows, and coordinating who is doing what, by when, and to what standard becomes increasingly difficult to manage through informal means.
Historically, and still today in many organizations, project and task coordination is carried out using spreadsheets, email threads, paper-based logs, and verbal or chat-based instructions. While these tools are familiar and require no specialized training, they do not scale well: spreadsheets are edited by one person at a time and quickly become outdated; email and chat messages bury task assignments and updates in unstructured conversation threads; and paper logs cannot be searched, shared in real time, or backed up reliably. As a direct consequence, tasks are duplicated or forgotten, deadlines are missed without early warning, accountability for specific pieces of work becomes unclear, and managers lack a single, trustworthy view of how a project is actually progressing.
The emergence of web-based information systems has offered a practical response to these coordination problems. A web-based Project and Task Management System (PTMS) allows an organization to centralize project data, assign and track tasks against specific individuals, attach relevant files and comments directly to the work they concern, and receive automated reminders as deadlines approach - all accessible from any device with a browser, without organization-wide software installation. Such systems also make it possible to enforce role-based access control (RBAC), so that Administrators, Project Managers and Team Members each see and can act on only the information relevant to their responsibilities, which improves both security and usability (Kruchten, 2019).
This study is motivated by the continued reliance of many organizations on informal, manual approaches to project and task coordination despite the availability of mature web technologies capable of solving the underlying problem. It responds to that gap by designing and implementing a role-based, web-based Project and Task Management System - built on ASP.NET Core 9 MVC, Entity Framework Core and Microsoft SQL Server, and structured according to Clean Architecture principles - that centralizes project and task administration, team assignment, deadline tracking, reporting and analytics, and audit logging in a single, secure platform.
1.2 Statement of the Problem
Despite growing awareness of formal project management practice, many small and medium-sized organizations continue to coordinate projects and tasks through disconnected, manual tools such as spreadsheets, verbal instructions and generic messaging applications. This creates several persistent problems. First, task ownership is often ambiguous: because assignments are not recorded in a structured, queryable system, it is difficult to determine, at any given moment, who is responsible for a specific task and what its current status is. Second, deadlines are tracked informally, so there is no automated mechanism to warn a Team Member, Project Manager or Administrator that a task is approaching or has passed its due date, which results in avoidable delays. Third, supporting materials such as comments, clarifications and file attachments relevant to a task are scattered across email, chat applications and personal folders rather than being attached to the task itself, making it difficult to reconstruct the history and context of a piece of work. Fourth, managers lack real-time, consolidated visibility into project and team performance, since producing a status report typically requires manually compiling information from multiple disconnected sources. Finally, without centralized, role-based access control, sensitive project information can be seen or modified by individuals who should not have that level of access, and there is no reliable audit trail of who changed what and when.
These problems collectively reduce organizational productivity, delay project delivery, weaken accountability and increase the risk of miscommunication. It is against this background that this study designs and implements a role-based, web-based Project and Task Management System intended to address these specific shortcomings by centralizing project and task data, automating deadline tracking, attaching comments and files directly to tasks, enforcing role-based access control, providing real-time dashboards and exportable reports, and maintaining a full audit log of system activity.

CHAPTER TWO: LITERATURE REVIEW
This chapter reviews existing literature relevant to the design and implementation of the Project and Task Management System (PTMS). It is organized into three parts, in line with the East Africa University research guidelines: an overview of the key concepts and theories underlying project and task management systems; a review of literature specific to each of the study's three specific objectives; and a statement of the research gap that this study addresses.
2.1 Overview of Concepts and Related Theories
2.1.1 Project and Task Management
The Project Management Institute (2021) defines a project as a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service or result, and project management as the application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements. Kerzner (2017) extends this by framing project management as the planning, organizing, directing and controlling of company resources for a relatively short-term objective established to complete specific goals. Within a project, a task is the smallest unit of assignable work - a discrete piece of work with a defined owner, scope and, typically, a deadline - and task management is the process of tracking a task from creation through assignment, execution and completion (Kerzner, 2017). Effective project management therefore depends on effective task management at the operational level: a project can only be tracked accurately if the tasks that compose it are individually visible, assigned and status-tracked.
Traditional project management theory, exemplified by the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK), emphasizes formal process groups - initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing - supported by documented plans and schedules (Project Management Institute, 2021). While this theory provides a strong conceptual foundation, its practical application in small and medium-sized organizations is frequently informal: managers apply the underlying principles (defining scope, assigning ownership, tracking progress) without the heavyweight documentation the full standard implies. This is precisely the gap that lightweight, software-supported project and task management tools are designed to fill - by embedding the underlying principles of ownership, deadlines and progress tracking directly into a system's data model and workflow, rather than requiring separate paper-based process documentation.
2.1.2 Management Information Systems and Web-Based Applications
A Management Information System (MIS) is a computer-based system that provides managers with the tools to organize, evaluate and efficiently manage departments within an organization by collecting, processing, storing and disseminating information to support decision-making (Laudon & Laudon, 2020). A Project and Task Management System is a specialized category of MIS, focused specifically on project- and task-level operational data rather than organization-wide financial or resource data. Laudon and Laudon (2020) note that the value of an MIS lies not merely in digitizing existing manual processes, but in restructuring how information flows through an organization so that the right person has the right information, in a usable form, at the right time - which, for project and task management, means that task owners, managers and administrators must each see the subset of information relevant to their role, updated in real time as work progresses.
The shift from desktop, single-user software to web-based applications has been a defining trend in enterprise software over the past two decades. Fowler (2003) observes that web-based, multi-tier enterprise applications separate presentation, business logic and data-access concerns into distinct layers, which allows an application to be accessed from any device with a browser while remaining centrally maintained and updated. This architectural shift is directly relevant to project and task management: a web-based PTMS allows Administrators, Project Managers and Team Members in different physical locations to view and update the same project data concurrently, without installing or synchronizing separate copies of the application, addressing one of the core weaknesses of spreadsheet-based coordination identified in Chapter One.
2.1.3 Role-Based Access Control
Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), as formalized by Sandhu, Coyne, Feinstein and Youman (1996), is an access control paradigm in which permissions are associated with roles, and users are assigned to appropriate roles, rather than permissions being assigned to individual users directly. Ferraiolo and Kuhn's subsequent work on the RBAC model, later adopted as an ANSI/INCITS standard, formalized role hierarchies and separation-of-duty constraints that allow complex organizational permission structures to be expressed and enforced consistently (Ferraiolo, Sandhu, Gavrila, Kuhn & Chandramouli, 2001). In the context of a project and task management system, RBAC allows the system to enforce, in code, the principle that an Administrator has full system-wide access, a Project Manager has full control only over the projects and teams they manage, and a Team Member can view and update only the tasks assigned to them - without relying on user discipline or manual convention to keep users from exceeding their intended scope of action. Modern web frameworks, including the ASP.NET Core Identity system used in this study, implement RBAC as a first-class feature, associating users with named roles and allowing controller actions and views to be restricted by role or by a finer-grained authorization policy (Microsoft, 2024).
2.1.4 Software Development Life Cycle and Agile Methodology
The Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is a structured process for planning, creating, testing and deploying an information system (Sommerville, 2016). Traditional SDLC models, such as the Waterfall model, treat requirements, design, implementation, testing and deployment as sequential, largely non-overlapping phases. Sommerville (2016) notes that this sequential approach performs poorly when requirements are likely to change during development, as is common in small-team, evolving software projects. In response, Agile methodologies, formalized in the Agile Manifesto (Beck et al., 2001), favor iterative and incremental development, in which working software is delivered in short cycles, feedback is incorporated continuously, and requirements are allowed to evolve as understanding of the problem deepens. Pressman and Maxim (2020) describe incremental development as particularly well suited to systems, such as management information systems, whose core modules (in this case, user/role management, project management, and task management) can be delivered and validated independently before dependent modules (comments, attachments, notifications, reporting, analytics) are layered on top.
This study adopts an Agile-oriented, incremental SDLC precisely because the PTMS was developed as a set of increasingly complex, testable modules - identity and role management first, then project and task CRUD, then team assignment, then comments/attachments/notifications, then reporting/analytics/audit logging - with each module unit-tested before the next was layered on top, consistent with the incremental model described by Pressman and Maxim (2020).
2.1.5 Clean Architecture and Layered Software Design
Martin (2018) proposes Clean Architecture as a set of design principles intended to produce systems that are independent of frameworks, independent of the user interface, independent of the database, and therefore easier to test and to maintain over time. The central principle, the Dependency Rule, states that source-code dependencies must point only inward, toward higher-level policy, so that inner layers (business entities and use cases) have no knowledge of outer layers (frameworks, databases, web delivery mechanisms). Fowler (2003) similarly advocates a layered architecture - separating presentation, domain logic and data-source layers - as a way of containing the impact of change: a change to the database schema should not require changes to business rules, and a change to the user interface should not require changes to the database access code.
This study applies Clean Architecture directly: the system is organized into a Domain layer (entities and enumerations with no external dependencies), an Application layer (DTOs and service interfaces expressing business rule contracts), an Infrastructure layer (Entity Framework Core, ASP.NET Core Identity, repositories and concrete service implementations), and a Web layer (MVC controllers, views and view models, acting as the composition root), with dependencies flowing Web ? Infrastructure ? Application ? Domain, so that the Domain and Application layers have no dependency on the web framework or the database technology in use, as recommended by Martin (2018).
2.1.6 Software Testing
Sommerville (2016) distinguishes between unit testing, which verifies that an individual component behaves correctly in isolation, and integration testing, which verifies that components function correctly when combined. Pressman and Maxim (2020) argue that automated unit and integration tests, run repeatedly as a codebase evolves, are a primary defense against regression - the reintroduction of previously fixed defects when new code is added. In the .NET ecosystem, the xUnit framework is widely used for automated unit testing, and ASP.NET Core's WebApplicationFactory enables full-stack integration testing of an MVC application, including its routing, middleware and database access, without requiring a deployed instance of the application (Microsoft, 2024). This study applies both testing levels: unit tests exercise individual services (such as project, task, team and attachment services) against an in-memory database, while integration tests exercise the application end-to-end through WebApplicationFactory against a dedicated, disposable SQL Server database.
2.2 Review of Specific Objectives of the Study
2.2.1 Requirements Analysis for Role-Based Systems (Objective One)
Requirements analysis is the process of determining the needs and expectations of a new or modified system's stakeholders (Sommerville, 2016). For a role-based system, requirements analysis must explicitly identify the distinct needs of each user role: Kulak and Guiney (2012) argue that failing to separate stakeholder requirements by role is a common cause of systems that are either too permissive (exposing sensitive functions to all users) or too restrictive (blocking legitimate users from functions they need). Entity-relationship modeling, as formalized by Chen (1976), remains the standard technique for translating requirements into a data model, by identifying entities (such as User, Project, Task), their attributes, and the relationships (one-to-many, many-to-many) among them - a technique this study applies directly in Chapter Three to model the relationships among users, projects, tasks, comments, attachments, notifications and audit logs.
2.2.2 Design and Development of Web-Based Management Systems (Objective Two)
Literature on the design and development of web-based management information systems consistently emphasizes the separation of concerns achieved through the Model-View-Controller (MVC) pattern, in which the Model represents application data and business rules, the View renders the user interface, and the Controller mediates user input and coordinates between the two (Fowler, 2003). Microsoft's ASP.NET Core MVC framework implements this pattern for .NET web applications, and is combined in this study with Entity Framework Core, an Object-Relational Mapper (ORM) that allows the Domain layer's plain C# entity classes to be persisted to and retrieved from a relational database (SQL Server) without hand-written SQL for routine operations (Microsoft, 2024). Ambler and Sadalage (2006) note that when an ORM and a relational database are combined with a version-controlled migration mechanism - as Entity Framework Core provides through its Migrations feature - the evolution of the database schema can be tracked and reproduced alongside the application's source code, which is the approach followed in this study's Infrastructure layer.
2.2.3 Testing and Evaluation of Software Systems (Objective Three)
Literature on software quality assurance holds that a system's correctness cannot be assumed from its design alone and must be empirically verified (Pressman & Maxim, 2020). The test pyramid concept, popularized by Cohn (2009), recommends a large base of fast unit tests, a smaller layer of integration tests, and a still smaller layer of end-to-end tests, on the basis that unit tests are cheapest to write and fastest to run, while integration and end-to-end tests, though slower, are needed to catch defects that arise only from the interaction of multiple components. This study's testing strategy - unit tests against an in-memory database for individual services, and integration tests via WebApplicationFactory against a real, disposable SQL Server database - follows this pyramid structure, and is consistent with the evaluation approaches described by Sommerville (2016) for verifying that a system meets its functional requirements before being considered complete.
2.3 Research Gap
The literature reviewed above establishes, individually, the theoretical foundations of project and task management, role-based access control, layered/Clean Architecture, and structured software testing. However, much of the published literature on project management tools evaluates commercial, closed-source platforms (such as large proprietary project-management suites) from a user-adoption or organizational-behavior perspective, rather than documenting the end-to-end design and implementation of an original, role-based system built from first principles using a specific, verifiable architectural approach. Similarly, while Clean Architecture (Martin, 2018) is well documented as a general design philosophy, there is comparatively little academic literature - particularly within Somali and East African higher-education research - that applies it concretely to a role-based project and task management domain and documents the resulting entity model, service layer and test strategy in detail.
This study addresses that gap by producing a fully documented, from-first-principles design and implementation of a role-based, web-based Project and Task Management System, built on Clean Architecture and validated through both unit and integration testing, with the system's own codebase - its entities, services, controllers and tests - serving as the primary evidentiary basis for Chapters Three and Four. In doing so, the study contributes a concrete, reproducible case study connecting established project-management and software-engineering theory to a working, testable software artifact, which is an area the reviewed literature leaves comparatively underexplored.
CHAPTER THREE: RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
This chapter presents the methodology followed in conducting this study. It covers the research design, the study population, the sample size and sampling technique, the data collection instruments, the system development methodology (including requirements analysis, system architecture, database design and use-case design), the data presentation and analysis tools, and the research ethics observed throughout the study.
3.1 Research Design
This study adopted a design science / applied system-development research design, combined with a descriptive component for requirements gathering. A design science approach is appropriate when the primary outcome of a study is a working artifact - in this case, a software system - developed to solve a defined, real-world problem and evaluated against defined objectives (Sommerville, 2016). The descriptive component involved reviewing documented organizational project-management practices and existing literature (Chapter Two) to identify the functional and non-functional requirements that the system needed to satisfy.
On the technical side, the study adopted an Agile-oriented, incremental Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC). The system was built as a sequence of independently testable increments - (1) identity, roles and authentication; (2) project and task management; (3) team assignment; (4) comments, attachments and notifications; (5) deadline tracking, reporting, analytics and audit logging - with each increment unit-tested before the next was added, consistent with the incremental development model described by Pressman and Maxim (2020). This design was chosen over a strict Waterfall approach because it allowed requirements for later modules (for example, the exact fields needed on a notification) to be refined based on what was learned while building earlier modules (for example, the task status-change workflow), which is the central justification Sommerville (2016) gives for preferring iterative models on systems of this size.
3.2 Study Population
The population relevant to this study consists of the three categories of end-user the system is designed to serve within any adopting organization: Administrators (who configure the system, manage users and view organization-wide data), Project Managers (who create and manage projects, assign tasks and team members, and monitor team performance), and Team Members (who execute assigned tasks, update task status and progress, and communicate through comments and attachments). These three categories were also used, throughout requirements analysis and system design, as the study's three primary system actors (see Section 3.5.4), since the system's functional requirements and access-control rules are defined directly in terms of what each of these three roles is permitted to do.
3.3 Sample Size and Sampling Technique
Because this is a system-development study rather than a large-scale survey, requirements were not drawn from a statistically representative sample of an external population. Instead, purposive sampling was used to identify a small number of key informants - individuals with direct experience coordinating projects and tasks manually (for example, using spreadsheets or messaging applications) - whose described pain points informed the problem statement in Chapter One and the functional requirements in Section 3.5.1. Purposive sampling is appropriate here because the goal was not statistical generalization but depth of insight into concrete, recurring coordination problems that a role-based system could plausibly resolve (Sommerville, 2016). For the testing and evaluation phase (Objective Three), the "sample" consisted of the system's own functional modules - every service class and controller action implemented - each of which was targeted by at least one automated unit or integration test, so that test coverage, rather than respondent count, defines the sample for that phase of the study.
3.4 Data Collection Instruments
• Document review: Existing literature on project management, RBAC, Clean Architecture and SDLC models (Chapter Two), together with common organizational artifacts such as task spreadsheets and status-update email threads, were reviewed to identify recurring coordination problems.
• Informal key-informant discussion: Short, unstructured discussions with individuals experienced in manual project/task coordination were used to validate that the problems identified in the literature matched practical experience, and to surface specific features (e.g. file attachments on tasks, deadline reminders) that respondents considered valuable.
• System/code-based instruments: For Objective Three, automated unit tests (xUnit, against an Entity Framework Core InMemory database) and integration tests (ASP.NET Core WebApplicationFactory, against a dedicated, disposable SQL Server database) served as the primary data-collection instruments, producing pass/fail evidence for each tested behavior.

3.5.2 System Architecture Design
The system was designed according to Clean Architecture (Martin, 2018), organized into four projects with dependencies flowing strictly inward, as illustrated in Figure 3.1. 4.1 Introduction
This chapter presents, analyses and discusses the findings of the study, organised objective by objective in line with the specific objectives stated in Section 1.4 and the design science / applied system-development methodology described in Chapter Three. Because the study is a system-development research project rather than a survey-based study, the data analysed in this chapter differs in kind from a conventional questionnaire-based study: it comprises the qualitative requirements data produced during document review and key-informant discussion (Section 3.3), the design and implementation artefacts of the Project and Task Management System (PTMS) itself, and the pass/fail evidence generated by the automated unit and integration tests described in Section 3.4 and Section 3.6.
Section 4.2 presents an overview of the basis for the analysis that follows. Sections 4.3 to 4.5 present and discuss the findings for Objective One, Objective Two and Objective Three respectively, in each case linking the findings back to the literature reviewed in Chapter Two. 4.2 Overview of the Basis for Analysis

4.3 Objective One: Findings on Requirements Analysis
The first objective of the study was to analyze the requirements of a role-based project and task management system, including the functional needs of Administrators, Project Managers and Team Members and the data relationships among users, projects, tasks, comments, attachments and notifications. The findings show that every functional requirement identified during requirements analysis (Table 3.1) could be attributed to at least one of the three system actors defined in Section 3.2, and that no requirement required access from an actor outside its assigned scope. This confirms, in practice, the caution raised by Kulak and Guiney (2012) that failing to separate stakeholder requirements by role is a common cause of systems that are either too permissive or too restrictive: because FR1-FR9 were tagged to specific actors from the outset, the subsequent authorization design (Section 4.4) had an unambiguous specification to enforce.
The entity-relationship model presented in Table 3.3 and Figure 3.2, developed using the entity-relationship technique formalised by Chen (1976), was found to fully account for the data needed to satisfy every functional requirement: the ApplicationUser-Project-ProjectMember relationship satisfies FR2 and FR3; the ProjectTask entity and its associated TaskComment, TaskAttachment and TaskHistory entities satisfy FR4 and FR5; the Notification entity satisfies FR6; and the AuditLog entity satisfies FR8. No additional entities were required during implementation beyond those identified at the design stage, indicating that the requirements analysis and entity-relationship modelling exercise in Chapter Three was complete relative to the objectives it was intended to support.
The non-functional requirements in Table 3.2 were similarly found to be realised without requiring rework during implementation. NFR1 (security) is enforced through role- and policy-based authorization attributes on every controller action identified in Table 4.1; NFR4 (auditability) is realised through the dedicated AuditLog entity, which records the actor, action, affected entity and timestamp for every security-relevant operation; and NFR3 (reliability) is directly evidenced by the test results discussed in Section 4.5. This finding is consistent with Sommerville's (2016) view that non-functional requirements, when stated as specific, verifiable constraints rather than vague aspirations, can be validated directly against the finished system rather than left as unverified assumptions.
4.4 Objective Two: Findings on System Design and Development
The second objective of the study was to design and develop a web-based system, structured according to Clean Architecture, that provides project and task management, team assignment, deadline tracking, notifications, comments and file attachments, reporting/analytics, and audit logging. Table 4.2 summarises the modules that were implemented to satisfy this objective, organised by the five development increments described in Section 3.1 and mapped to the Clean Architecture layer(s) each module principally touches.

Findings confirm that the Dependency Rule central to Clean Architecture (Martin, 2018) was maintained throughout development: the Domain and Application layer projects compile with no project reference to Infrastructure or Web, so that entities such as Project and ProjectTask, and the service interfaces that express business rules about them, remain unaware of ASP.NET Core, Entity Framework Core or any other framework concern. This was verified directly by inspecting the .csproj project references of each of the four layers, which show dependencies flowing strictly inward as illustrated in Figure 3.1.
The layered separation also produced the change-containment benefit that Fowler (2003) attributes to multi-tier enterprise architecture. For example, the Global Search feature (FR9) was added as part of the fifth increment after the first four increments were already complete and tested; implementing it required a new SearchService in the Infrastructure layer and a corresponding SearchController and view in the Web layer, but no change whatsoever to the Domain entities or to the Application-layer interfaces of the already-completed modules. This is direct, practical evidence that a change to one concern - in this case, adding a cross-cutting search capability - did not require, and did not risk, changes to unrelated business rules already implemented and tested, which is precisely the property Clean Architecture and layered design are intended to provide.
At the presentation layer, the system provides three role-scoped dashboards consistent with FR1 and the access-control model described in Section 2.1.3: the Administrator dashboard presents organisation-wide metrics (total users, total projects, system-wide task-status breakdown and recent audit-log activity); the Project Manager dashboard presents the status, progress and team workload of the projects the manager owns, visualised using Chart.js as noted in Table 3.5; and the Team Member dashboard presents only the tasks assigned to that member, together with upcoming deadlines and unread notifications. Each dashboard is rendered from a distinct, strongly-typed ViewModel populated by role-aware queries in the Infrastructure layer, so that no view ever receives - and therefore cannot inadvertently disclose - data outside the scope of the requesting user's role.

The distribution of 61 unit tests to 45 integration tests is flatter than the classical test pyramid popularised by Cohn (2009), which recommends a large base of unit tests and a comparatively small layer of integration tests. This is explained by the nature of the system under test: a substantial share of its behaviour - role- and policy-based authorization, Entity Framework Core query composition, and cross-module workflows such as a task-status change triggering a notification and an audit-log entry in the same request - can only be meaningfully verified end-to-end through ASP.NET Core's WebApplicationFactory rather than in isolation. This finding is consistent with Sommerville's (2016) observation that integration testing is necessary specifically to catch defects that arise from the interaction of components rather than from any single component in isolation.
Testing was not merely confirmatory: it surfaced and led to the correction of concrete defects during development, consistent with the regression-defense role Pressman and Maxim (2020) assign to automated testing in incremental development. Two examples illustrate this. First, an early implementation of the deadline-notification service generated a new overdue-task notification every time the background deadline-check job ran while a task remained overdue, rather than a single notification per occurrence; an integration test that ran the check twice against the same overdue task exposed the duplication, and the service was corrected to first check for an existing unread notification of the same type before creating a new one. Second, an early implementation of project deletion performed a hard delete that cascaded to a project's tasks, comments and attachments; because this was found, on review against NFR4 (auditability), to destroy exactly the history that an audit trail is meant to preserve, the implementation was revised to a soft-delete pattern using an IsDeleted flag and a DeletedAt timestamp, paired with a corresponding AuditLog entry, and a dedicated integration test was added to confirm that a soft-deleted project's tasks remain retrievable for audit purposes while no longer appearing in normal project listings.
All 106 tests pass at the time of writing (100% pass rate), and the full suite - unit tests against an Entity Framework Core InMemory database and integration tests against a dedicated, disposable SQL Server database via WebApplicationFactory - is re-run before any further change is merged into the codebase, providing the regression safety net that both Sommerville (2016) and Pressman and Maxim (2020) identify as a precondition for evolving a system incrementally without reintroducing previously-fixed defects.
4.6 Discussion of Findings
Read together, the findings in Sections 4.3 to 4.5 answer the three research questions posed in Section 1.5. On the question of functional and data requirements, Section 4.3 shows that a complete, role-differentiated set of functional and non-functional requirements was derived and that the resulting entity-relationship model was sufficient, without later revision, to support every requirement through to implementation. On the question of how such a system can be designed and developed using Clean Architecture, Section 4.4 shows that the four-layer structure was maintained in practice (not only on paper), that it demonstrably contained the impact of at least one significant change (the addition of Global Search), and that it supported role-scoped presentation without leaking data across roles. On the question of the extent to which the developed system functions correctly, Section 4.5 shows that all 106 automated unit and integration tests pass, and that the testing process itself caught and corrected two non-trivial defects before they could reach a deployed system.
These findings also speak directly to the problem statement in Section 1.2 and the research gap identified in Section 2.3. Where Section 1.2 described ambiguous task ownership, informally tracked deadlines, scattered supporting materials, limited management visibility and weak access control as the consequences of manual coordination, the system's role-scoped task assignment (FR4), automated deadline notifications (FR6), task-attached comments and files (FR5), analytics dashboards (FR7) and role/policy-based authorization (NFR1) directly target each of these consequences, and their correct operation is the specific claim verified in Section 4.5. Where Section 2.3 noted a shortage of academic literature that documents, in reproducible detail, the application of Clean Architecture to a role-based project and task management domain, this chapter's traceability matrix (Table 4.1), module summary (Table 4.2) and test-result summary (Table 4.3) together constitute exactly that documentation, grounded in the system's own codebase rather than in a purely theoretical account of the architecture.
Overall, the findings support the conclusion that a centralized, role-based, Clean-Architecture web application can satisfy the functional and non-functional requirements of project and task coordination identified in this study, that the architecture's theoretical benefits (testability, change containment, independence from frameworks) are observable in the concrete development history of the system, and that the system's correctness, as far as automated testing can establish it, is well supported by the evidence presented in this chapter. Chapter Five draws on these findings to present the study's conclusions and recommendations.
